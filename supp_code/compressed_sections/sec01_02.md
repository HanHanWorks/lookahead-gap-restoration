## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Why a criterion, and not another comparison

Post-disaster restoration of a transmission network is a sequential decision problem: a limited set of switchable lines must be re-energized in an order that restores as much unserved load as possible, as early as possible. Machine learning has been applied to this ordering problem at a steady rate, and the typical study reports that its learned policy outperforms a set of reference rules on one or two published test systems.

Two questions precede that evidence, and both are usually left open. The first is **attribution**. When a learned policy outperforms a reference, which component produced the gain: the learned ordering, the admissibility screen that decides whether an action is legal, the aggregate metric, or the choice of reference? A policy whose gain comes entirely from refusing illegal actions is not evidence that learning orders anything. The second question is **answerability**. If at a given decision step every admissible action yields the same immediate benefit, then no ordering is better than any other at that step, and averages and significance tests computed over such steps describe the metric rather than the policies.

This paper takes the position that both questions can be answered *before* training, by an exactly computable quantity, which we develop, compute, and use to interpret an experimental programme on three public test systems. The resulting claims are about the structure of the prioritization problem, not the performance of any particular learned policy. Figure 1 collects the objects involved.

### 1.2 What is known, and what is missing

Three limitations recur in prior work on learned restoration prioritization.

**Limitation 1: reported gains are not attributed to a component.** A policy that screens inadmissible actions and one that does not are compared as if they differed only in their ordering, although they differ in two mechanisms at once.

**Limitation 2: benchmark conditions are not checked for identifiability.** Because the objective is defined over candidate actions, a condition in which the admissible candidates are tied carries no information about any ordering. Aggregating such conditions into a primary test family inflates the apparent evidence rather than adding to it.

**Limitation 3: the value of lookahead is reported, but not its accessibility or its regime.** A scalar measure of headroom does not say whether a *cheap* controller can reach it, nor what the service model must assume for the headroom to exist. A value that requires an intractable plan is not deployable, and a value that exists only under one capacity convention is a property of the convention as much as of the grid.

**Our goal**, in one sentence: *to provide an exactly computable criterion that separates how much lookahead is worth (a value) from how cheaply it can be realised (an accessibility), to establish the regime of the service model in which the value is non-trivial, and to demonstrate the criterion on three public test systems.*

### 1.3 The technical challenges

**Challenge 1: exact optimality is combinatorial.** The value of lookahead is naturally the difference between the best achievable horizon objective and the objective of the *one-step exact rule*, which repeatedly reconnects the admissible line of greatest immediate recovery. The best achievable objective ranges over orderings of the broken key lines, so an exact value appears to require simulation or intractable enumeration; without one, no statement about *any* policy is possible.

**Challenge 2: value and accessibility are different questions.** A scalar value measures how much is at stake, not whether a controller with bounded foresight can capture it. The two can diverge either way, and the divergence determines the practical recommendation, so separating them requires a construct indexed by the controller's foresight.

**Challenge 3: the value may be an artefact of the evaluator's convention.** The service functional here is a maximum-flow construct whose capacities are synthesised from a base-case direct-current power flow. Whether the resulting headroom reflects the power system or merely the capacity convention is not determined by the benchmark itself; establishing it requires varying the convention at fixed instances.

### 1.4 Contributions

- **C1 · An attribution framework** *(Section 3)*. Any prioritization policy is a ranker composed with an admissibility screen, and its stepwise regret decomposes exactly into a ranking term and a censoring term. A matched-training leave-one-out protocol that varies the training objective and the presence of the screen independently makes the source of a reported gain identifiable; Section 3.5 states where the release supports the indicator form only.

- **C2 · Exact solvability and the lookahead gap** *(Sections 4.1--4.3)*. Because the unserved-load functional depends only on the *set* of broken lines, terminal behaviour is order-invariant and the full-horizon optimum reduces to a dynamic program over subsets of the broken key lines, costing $O(2^{m}m)$ service evaluations and no simulation. The resulting gap exactly bounds the benefit of any prioritization policy over the one-step exact rule.

- **C3 · The depth profile and the accessibility of the gap** *(Sections 4.4--4.6)*. We distinguish a *committed* plan, which executes a $k$-step plan and then reverts, from a *rolling* (receding-horizon) controller, which replans at every step. A committed plan needs five steps of foresight to capture the gap, and without a budget captures only 21% of it at depth four; a rolling depth-2 controller captures 99.4--99.8% and depth 3 captures it exactly.

- **C4 · The service-model regime of the criterion** *(Section 6)*. At fixed instances the gap is positive when line capacities are proportional to base-case utilisation, and vanishes identically under uniform physical thermal ratings or a sufficiently large capacity floor. We also record why a direct optimal-power-flow transplant is not comparable.

- **C5 · A one-directional criterion and a complementary diagnostic** *(Sections 4.4, 5.1)*. Step-wise degeneracy implies a vanishing gap, but not conversely. Crossing the pre-check with the gap over eight condition groups shows the two to be complementary rather than equivalent.

A supporting contribution is a **reporting standard for latency claims** *(Section 7)*: any latency figure should be accompanied by the cheapest non-learned rule that could replace the learned one, and the measurement boundary should be stated, because the same trained network is measured 2.5--3.0 times slower when a deep-learning library's prediction wrapper is included than when a bare forward pass is timed.

### 1.5 Related work and the axes of novelty

Four branches are relevant, and the tension between the first two is what gives the criterion its point. Appendix E records the search protocol, the 41 verified works, and the directions in which nothing was retrieved.

**Learned restoration prioritization.** Reinforcement learning has been applied to restoration ordering across distribution and transmission systems, including graph-based outage management [1], multi-agent load restoration with invalid-action masking [2], mixed discrete-continuous actions over tie switches and distributed resources [3,4], graph-sequence scheduling [5] and entropy-driven coordination of storage with microgrids [6]; surveys converge on the absence of standardised benchmarks and reproducibility [7--9]. Two features matter here. The reported advantage of learning is conditional on the reference, holding against greedy, $\varepsilon$-greedy, Boltzmann or evolutionary baselines, and where an exact solver is included the learned policy is generally better in time and worse in quality [6]. And the primary evidence is an aggregate metric over one or two systems, with the component that produced the gain left unisolated.

**Classical restoration optimization.** The ROP and CRP line [10--15] formulates restoration as an ordering and routing problem, solves it with mixed-integer or nonlinear programming, and argues explicitly that fast intuitive heuristics yield low-quality plans [12]. It supplies the nearest neighbour to the present analysis — dynamic repair scheduling with a look-ahead approximation to the cost-to-go, in which depth trades efficiency against optimality [16] — alongside sequence-dependent repair durations [17], crew routing under time budgets [18--20] and formulations carrying explicit thermal and voltage constraints [19,21]. Its own flagship result, however, is a heuristic claimed to be near-optimal and a thousand times faster [12], which weakens the absoluteness of the *heuristics are insufficient* position and suggests the operating axis is the compute--quality trade-off rather than policy quality.

**Methodology, critique and adjacent theory.** Outside the power domain a mature literature documents the statistical fragility of method comparisons: seed lottery [22], protocol changes that reverse rankings [23], benchmark selection determining conclusions [24], oracle gaps revealing benchmarks that cannot separate generalisation from non-generalisation [25], and protocol-level identifiability audits [26--29]. The closest power-domain analogue links a proxy's reported accuracy to the sampling coverage of variability factors [30]. Adjacent theory supplies the guarantees the criterion is measured against: constant-factor bounds for adaptive greedy policies [31] and for myopic rules on stochastic depletion problems [32,33], the approximation theory of minimum-latency scheduling [34,35], rollout and policy iteration [36], and queueing with future information [37]. Cold-load pickup [38] and protection coordination under reconfiguration [39--41] are engineering constraints a synthetic-capacity benchmark omits.

**The tension, and what is missing.** The classical branch argues heuristics are insufficient; the learning branch argues learning is better. Both compete on the compute--quality trade-off, and the premise of that competition, namely that the benchmark contains benefit that *some* policy could capture, is never checked. This paper supplies the check, and a structured search confirmed five specific gaps (Appendix E). No prior work treats the capacity or constraint *rule* of a service model as the thing that decides whether a positive gap exists [30 is the nearest analogue, for a different object]. None defines an exactly computable myopic-versus-optimal gap for power restoration [16 is the nearest, and is approximate]. None distinguishes a committed depth-$k$ profile from a rolling controller's realised profile. None applies the minimum-latency theory of [34,35] to restoration ordering. And none proposes a *decision-landscape* identifiability criterion, since the existing literature asks whether observations or designs can separate parameters [26--29] rather than whether an optimisation landscape contains capturable benefit. Against the constant-factor guarantees of [31--33] the criterion is complementary rather than competing: those bounds are instance-independent and cannot answer whether *this* benchmark is worth learning, whereas the gap is instance-exact by construction (Section 4.4).

Section 2 fixes the notation and the service model, Sections 3 and 4 develop the theory, Sections 5 and 6 verify it, and Sections 7 to 10 state the reporting standard, draw the consequences, record the boundaries and conclude.

## 2. Problem formulation

### 2.1 System and decision objects

Let $\mathcal{L}$ be the set of lines of a transmission network and $\mathcal{K} \subset \mathcal{L}$ the set of *switchable key lines*, $|\mathcal{K}| = n_a$, identified by a line-importance screen; for the test systems used here $n_a = 20$. A scenario samples an initial contingency: a set $B_0 \subseteq \mathcal{K}$ of broken key lines and a set $O_0 \subseteq \mathcal{L} \setminus \mathcal{K}$ of broken non-key lines, with $m = |B_0|$.

An episode runs for $H = 25$ decision steps. At step $t$ the policy selects an action $a \in \mathcal{K}$. If $a$ is still broken it is reconnected and the reconnected set becomes $S_{t} = S_{t-1} \cup \{a\}$; otherwise the action has no effect and the step is consumed. The set of broken lines at step $t$ is therefore $(B_0 \setminus S_t) \cup O_0$.

### 2.2 Service model and the capacity rule

Unserved load is evaluated by a maximum-flow construct on a graph whose nodes are buses and whose edges are the in-service lines and transformers. Writing $g(S)$ for the maximum flow that reaches the load nodes when the reconnected set is $S$, the unserved load and the recovery are

$$\ell(S) = \max\{0,\; g(\emptyset) - g(S)\}, \qquad r(S) = 100 \cdot \frac{\ell_0 - \ell(S)}{\ell_0}, \qquad \ell_0 = \ell(B_0 \cup O_0). \tag{1}$$

**The capacity rule is part of the service model, and we state it explicitly**, because Section 6 shows the criterion's non-triviality depends on it. Generator, storage and external-grid injections enter as source-edge capacities; each load bus is a sink edge of its demand. Line capacities follow the *utilisation-proportional* rule used by this benchmark family:

$$c_\ell = \max\bigl\{\kappa \cdot |f_\ell^{(0)}|,\; \phi \textstyle\sum_d d\bigr\}, \tag{2}$$

where $f_\ell^{(0)}$ is the base-case direct-current flow on line $\ell$ on the intact network, $\kappa$ a capacity margin, $\phi$ a floor share, and $\sum_d d$ the total demand. Eq. (2) ties each line's capacity to its base-case utilisation, with a floor preventing a lightly loaded line from being closed outright. Section 6 varies this rule; Table 1 records the parameters of both configurations.

### 2.3 Objective

Let $S_t$ be the reconnected set after step $t$. An action on a line that is not broken changes no set and consumes the step; call the step **ineffective**, write $\tilde m(\pi) \le m$ for the number of effective actions, and index effective steps in order. The recorded objective is the area under the recovery curve over the horizon,

$$J(\pi) \;=\; \sum_{t=1}^{\tilde m(\pi)} r(S_t) \;+\; \bigl(H - \tilde m(\pi) - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr)\, r\bigl(S_{\tilde m(\pi)}\bigr), \tag{3}$$

in recovery-percent $\times$ step units, referred to throughout as AUC@25; the trapezoidal weight applies to the terminal set because it is held to the horizon.

**Assumption A0.** An ineffective step realises zero recovery and leaves the state unchanged. This is a property of the environment rather than a restriction on policies: by Eq. (1) the unserved load is a function of the *set* of broken lines, and an ineffective action does not alter that set.

> **Lemma 0 (effective steps should be taken first).** Under A0, with $H \ge m$, every policy that completes the reconnection within the horizon satisfies $J(\pi) \le \sum_{t=1}^{m} r(S_t) + (H - m - \tfrac{1}{2})\, r(S_m)$, with equality if and only if it takes no ineffective step.

*Proof.* The recovery curve of $\pi$ is $r(S_1) \le \cdots \le r(S_m)$ with one repetition of an earlier value in each ineffective position. Moving the effective action at any later position $t' > t$ into an ineffective $t$ and shifting the intervening values left leaves every shifted value unchanged, places at $t$ a value at least as large as the one it displaces, and fixes the horizon, so the trapezoidal area does not decrease; repeating until no ineffective position remains proves the inequality, and the equality condition follows because the repetition is strictly smaller than the value it displaces whenever the chain has not terminated. $\square$

Lemma 0 lets the rest of the paper treat the objective as a function of the *chain* of reconnected sets: an optimal policy takes no ineffective step, and one that does only loses. The phenomenon is not marginal, 51.1% of the 147,132 recorded decision steps of the main protocol being ineffective and 4,026 of 9,600 episodes containing at least one (Section 5.3). Because $S_m = B_0$ is the same set for every policy that reconnects all broken key lines within the horizon, the terminal term of the bound is order-invariant, which Section 4.1 makes the basis of the analysis.

### 2.4 Policies as ranker composed with screen

Restoration benchmarks differ not only in how they order actions but in whether they restrict the action set, and the two mechanisms are usually confounded. We therefore write every policy in the class as a composition

$$\pi = \rho \circ \varphi, \qquad \varphi : \mathcal{S} \times \mathcal{K} \to \{0,1\}, \qquad \rho : \mathcal{S} \to \text{total order on } \mathcal{K}, \tag{4}$$

which executes the admissibility-maximal action according to $\rho$. A learned ranker without a screen has $\varphi \equiv 1$; the same ranker with a screen has $\varphi$ equal to the indicator that the line is still broken; deterministic references such as the electrical-priority and risk-only rules are a fixed $\rho$ with $\varphi \equiv 1$. Section 3 shows this decomposition is exactly additive in regret.

### 2.5 Statistical unit

Scenarios are the independent unit. Learnable policies are trained under several seeds; for a fixed scenario the seeds are first aggregated within the policy, and only then are policies compared across scenarios by paired tests. Treating a (seed, scenario) pair as independent would overstate the evidence. Family-wise control within a group of comparisons uses Holm's step-down procedure.

### 2.6 Identifiability pre-check

Because the metric is defined on candidate actions, a condition in which the admissible candidates are tied contains no information about any ordering. Before any comparison we therefore report three diagnostics per condition: the fraction of scenarios with positive initial unserved load; the fraction of decision steps at which the maximizing candidate is unique; and the ratio of the largest single-action recovery gain to the initial unserved load. The last two bear on identifiability for different reasons: a low fraction of unique maximizers means most steps present a tie and cannot reward a correct order, while a ratio close to one means a single action recovers almost everything, leaving no ordering decision anything to distribute.

**A condition is declared non-identifiable, and excluded from the primary test family, when the maximizing candidate is unique at fewer than 60% of its decision steps, or when a single admissible action recovers more than 85% of the initial unserved load.** The thresholds are stated because a criterion of this kind is only as useful as its cut-off is explicit; they were chosen so that the two diagnostics agree on the cases examined below and separate those cases widely (Section 5.1). Non-identifiable conditions are reported separately, since their means and significance tests measure the metric rather than the policies.

Both legacy IEEE 118-bus groups fail this pre-check on both diagnostics while both IEEE 300-bus groups pass (Section 5.1), so the failure is a property of the action set rather than of the test system or the problem; after the action-set correction all twelve conditions pass. The pre-check is also the first application of the criterion of Section 4: a failing pre-check implies a vanishing gap, though the converse fails, and Section 5.1 tabulates the joint distribution of the two diagnoses over all eight condition groups.
