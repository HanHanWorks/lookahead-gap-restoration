## 3. An attribution framework for prioritization policies

Section 1 identified the attribution gap: reported gains are not assigned to the mechanism that produces them. This section supplies the assignment. The construction is elementary, but it is what makes the later results interpretable, because every comparison in Section 5 is stated in its terms.

### 3.1 Stepwise benefit and the oracle action

Fix a scenario and a decision step $t$ with reconnected set $S_{t-1}$. For an admissible candidate $a$, let $u_t(a) = r(S_{t-1} \cup \{a\})$ be the recovery realized by reconnecting $a$, evaluated exactly by the service model of Section 2.2. Define the **oracle action** $a^\star_t = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{K}} u_t(a)$, taking the first maximizer when the maximum is not unique, and write $u^\star_t = u_t(a^\star_t)$.

Two properties are used repeatedly. First, $u^\star_t$ depends only on the *set* $\{S_{t-1} \cup \{a\} : a \in \mathcal{K}\}$, not on the policy, so it is a property of the instance rather than of the comparison. Second, when the maximum is not unique the step carries no ordering information; this is the per-step form of the identifiability condition of Section 2.6, counted explicitly in Section 5.

### 3.2 The regret identity

Consider a policy $\pi = \rho \circ \varphi$ as decomposed in Eq. (4). Write $\mathcal{A}_t = \{a \in \mathcal{K} : \varphi(S_{t-1}, a) = 1\}$ for the admissible set, $a^\rho_t = \operatorname{first}_{\rho}(\mathcal{A}_t)$ for the action the ranker favors *among the admissible ones*, and $\hat a_t$ for the action the policy **actually executes**. The three objects are distinct, and the distinction is what the identity below measures: a ranker evaluated without a screen executes its unconditional favorite, which need not lie in $\mathcal{A}_t$. Define the stepwise regret $\delta_t = u^\star_t - u_t(\hat a_t) \ge 0$; when $\mathcal{A}_t$ is empty the step is ineffective and $\delta_t$ is defined to be zero.

> **Proposition 1 (additive regret decomposition).** For every scenario and step with $\mathcal{A}_t \neq \emptyset$,
> $$\delta_t \;=\; \underbrace{\bigl(u^\star_t - u_t(a^\rho_t)\bigr)}_{\text{ranking error } R_t} \;+\; \underbrace{\bigl(u_t(a^\rho_t) - u_t(\hat a_t)\bigr)}_{\text{censoring loss } C_t}, \tag{5}$$
> with $R_t = 0$ exactly when the ranker places an oracle action first, and $C_t = 0$ exactly when the executed action is the ranker's admissible pick, $\hat a_t = a^\rho_t$.

*Proof.* The identity is an algebraic retelescoping of $u^\star_t - u_t(\hat a_t)$ through the intermediate value $u_t(a^\rho_t)$. For $R_t \ge 0$, use that $u^\star_t$ maximises $u_t$ over $\mathcal{K} \supseteq \mathcal{A}_t$ while $a^\rho_t \in \mathcal{A}_t$. For $C_t \ge 0$, note that $\hat a_t$ is either $a^\rho_t$, giving $C_t = 0$, or an inadmissible action, which by A0 realises zero recovery, so that $C_t = u_t(a^\rho_t) \ge 0$. $\square$

Aggregating over steps and scenarios gives the regret as the sum of two terms, which is useful only if the terms are separately measurable. The **indicator forms** are

$$C_t \;=\; \mathbf{1}\bigl[\hat a_t \notin \mathcal{A}_t\bigr], \qquad
D_t \;=\; \mathbf{1}\bigl[\hat a_t \neq a^\rho_t\bigr], \tag{5a}$$

with $\mathbf{1}[\cdot]$ the indicator of an event, so that $C_t$ records an executed action that was inadmissible and $D_t$ one that deviated from the ranker's admissible pick. On the released traces the two coincide: both are zero for the screened arms by construction, and $0.766$ and $0.758$ for the two unscreened arms (Section 5.3). The **value forms** of Eq. (5) additionally require $u_t(a^\rho_t)$, the one-step recovery of the ranker's admissible pick. That quantity is computed at run time but is not persisted for the unscreened arms, whose trajectories diverge from the screened ones at the first ineffective step and so cannot be joined to recover it; Section 3.5 records this as a measurement boundary and Appendix D as a release requirement. Figure 1(ii) states the identity in diagram form.

### 3.3 Why the screen is a mechanism and not a detail

Proposition 1 makes precise the statement in Limitation 1. A learned ranker evaluated without a screen accumulates censoring loss at every step where its preferred action is already reconnected; the same ranker with the screen has $C_t = 0$ by construction in Eq. (5). If the benchmark compares screened learning against unscreened references, or reports a single aggregate number, the two mechanisms move together and the source of the gain is unidentifiable.

Three derived quantities are therefore reported per policy, and the names are kept distinct. **Ranking fidelity** is the fraction of decision steps at which the ranker's order places an oracle action first, restricted to the admissible set. The **censoring rate** $C_t$ is the fraction of steps at which the executed action is inadmissible, Eq. (5a) — the mechanism a screen exists to remove. The **conditional screen override** $C^{\mathrm{screen}}_t$ is the fraction of steps at which the ranker's admissible pick *was* an oracle action and was nonetheless not executed; it is conditional, bounded above by the censoring rate and roughly half its size here. Reporting $C^{\mathrm{screen}}_t$ in place of $C_t$ understates the mechanism by a factor of about two, and is not done here.

### 3.4 Matched-training leave-one-out protocol

To separate the two mechanisms *causally* rather than by decomposition alone, four arms are trained under identical state representation, action set, scenario set and budget: a surrogate-objective ranker and a true-objective ranker, each with and without the admissibility screen at evaluation. Because the arms share everything except the training objective and the presence of the screen, an arm minus its screened counterpart isolates the censoring term, and the two objectives at fixed screening isolate the ranking term. The protocol is what makes the decomposition falsifiable, and it transfers unchanged to any study comparing a learned ranker against a non-learned reference.

### 3.5 What the framework does not claim

The decomposition is a bookkeeping identity: it says where a deficit sits, not that either term could be reduced. Whether the ranking term *can* be made small by learning is what Section 4 answers, and the answer is a property of the instance; Section 4 supplies the bound $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$ against which a policy's advantage over the one-step exact rule must be judged.

Two boundaries are stated rather than left implicit. First, **the identity is exact but the release is not complete**: the value forms of Eq. (5) need $u_t(a^\rho_t)$, which the released traces do not persist for the unscreened arms, so Section 5.3 reports the indicator forms of Eq. (5a) and marks which entries are measured and which hold by construction. Second, **the identity is conditional on A0**: on a step where the executed action is inadmissible the realised recovery is zero, so the censoring term carries the whole stepwise regret. Both boundaries follow from the environment and the release, not from the framework; the decomposition itself is retained, because $C_t$ is not identically zero (Section 5.3).

## 4. Exact solvability, the lookahead gap, and its depth profile

This section is the theoretical core: Sections 4.1--4.2 remove the terminal term from the ordering problem, Section 4.3 makes the full-horizon optimum exactly computable and defines the lookahead gap, Section 4.4 turns the gap into a benefit bound and an identifiability test, Section 4.5 separates the gap's *value* from its *accessibility*, and Section 4.6 closes with a cost-aware decision procedure.

### 4.1 Lemma 1: terminal behaviour is order-invariant

> **Lemma 1 (terminal invariance).** Fix a scenario. If two policies reconnect every broken key line within the horizon, then their terminal unserved load, terminal recovery and any terminal-only functional coincide, independently of the reconnection order.

*Proof.* Reconnection removes one element of $B_0$ per effective action, so after $m$ effective actions the reconnected set is $B_0$ under either policy. By Eq. (1) the unserved load depends on the policy's history only through the broken set $(B_0 \setminus S) \cup O_0$, which equals $O_0$ in both cases. Hence $\ell$, $r$ and any functional of them agree at the terminal step. $\square$

Two consequences make the lemma worth stating. A terminal metric cannot separate prioritization orders, which is why the objective of Section 2.3 is dominated by its transient term and why endpoint values reported in this benchmark family are typically identical across policies. And the ordering problem is thereby identified for what it is: not a choice of final state, but a choice of *sequence of intermediate sets*.

### 4.2 Reduction of the objective

Applying Lemma 1 to Eq. (3), the terminal term $(H - m - \tfrac12) r(B_0)$ is invariant across all policies that complete the reconnection within the horizon. Maximizing $J$ is therefore equivalent to maximizing the transient sum

$$\sum_{t=1}^{m} r(S_t) \tag{6}$$

over the sequence of intermediate sets. The problem is thus a *nested set-scheduling* problem: order $B_0$ to maximize the cumulative recovery accumulated along the chain $\emptyset \subset S_1 \subset \cdots \subset S_m = B_0$. No dynamic-feasibility consideration enters the objective, because the service model is a function of the broken set alone.

Resource coupling breaks this reduction, and we use it deliberately in Section 5.4. If a budget caps the number of effective actions at $B < m$, the terminal set is no longer $B_0$ but the policy's chosen subset of size $B$, the terminal term becomes order-dependent, and the nested-chain structure is replaced by a selection *and* sequencing problem — the subject of Corollary 4(b).

### 4.3 Proposition 2: the optimum is exactly computable without simulation

Let $f(S)$ denote the maximum additional recovery obtainable from reconnected set $S$ onward, with $S \subseteq B_0$. Since the objective of Section 4.2 depends only on $S$, the state is fully described by the reconnected set.

> **Proposition 2 (exact solvability by subset dynamic programming).** With $f(B_0) = 0$ and, for $S \subsetneq B_0$,
> $$f(S) \;=\; \max_{a \in B_0 \setminus S}\Bigl[\, r\bigl(S \cup \{a\}\bigr) + f\bigl(S \cup \{a\}\bigr) \Bigr], \tag{7}$$
> the value $f(\emptyset)$ equals the maximum of the transient sum of Eq. (6) over all orderings of $B_0$, and is attained by the ordering that realizes each maximizing branch. The computation requires $O(2^{m}\, m)$ evaluations of the service functional and no episode simulation.

*Proof.* Every ordering of $B_0$ corresponds to a maximal chain $\emptyset \subset S_1 \subset \cdots \subset S_m = B_0$, and the transient sum along that chain equals $f(\emptyset)$ as evaluated by backward induction, since $f$ satisfies the principle of optimality on the chain lattice. Conversely, each maximal chain is realized by at least one ordering, and following the arg-max branches of $f$ from $\emptyset$ constructs one. The state count is the number of subsets of $B_0$, the transition count at most $m$ per state, and each transition costs one service evaluation. $\square$

A quantity that appears to require simulating every ordering is therefore available exactly, at a cost of $2^m m$ cheap evaluations, on any instance with $m \le 12$. Section 5.4 reports the implementation check, and Appendix B names the artifact behind each record: the recursion reproduces the frozen gap values to $0.000000000$ on both protocols, the injected-capacity implementation reproduces them to $0.000000$ on all 40 shared instances, and the rolling controller stays inside the envelope $[J(\pi^{\mathrm{my}}), f(\emptyset)]$ that the recursion defines.

> **Definition (lookahead gap).** With $S^{\mathrm{my}}_t$ the chain generated by the one-step exact rule, the lookahead gap is
> $$\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}} \;=\; f(\emptyset) \;-\; \sum_{t=1}^{m} r\bigl(S^{\mathrm{my}}_t\bigr), \tag{8}$$
> measured in AUC@25 units.

$\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$ is the cumulative recovery a one-step exact rule forfeits relative to the best possible ordering. It is a property of the instance, computed without training and without knowing which policy will be evaluated.

### 4.4 Three consequences

> **Corollary 2 (the gap bounds every prioritization policy).** For any policy $\pi$ that completes the reconnection within the horizon, $J(\pi) \le J(\pi^{\mathrm{my}}) + \Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$. In particular, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}} = 0$ implies no policy can strictly improve on the one-step exact rule.

*Proof.* By Section 4.2, $J(\pi) - J(\pi^{\mathrm{my}}) = \sum_t r(S^\pi_t) - \sum_t r(S^{\mathrm{my}}_t) \le f(\emptyset) - \sum_t r(S^{\mathrm{my}}_t) = \Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$ by Eq. (8), the inequality following from Proposition 2. $\square$

> **Corollary 3 (the gap is an identifiability test, in one direction only).** $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}} = 0$ if and only if the one-step exact rule is optimal for the instance, by Eq. (8). It follows that *step-wise degeneracy implies a vanishing gap*: if at every decision step all admissible candidates yield the same one-step recovery, then every ordering accumulates the same sum and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}} = 0$. **The converse does not hold.**

The converse fails on this paper's own data. Under the legacy protocol all four condition groups have $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}} = 0$ to machine precision (maximum $1.1\times10^{-13}$), yet the maximizing candidate is unique at 95.4% and 100% of the decision steps of the two IEEE 300-bus groups (Table 2). A condition can therefore present a single best immediate action at almost every step and still leave the greedy rule globally optimal, because a correct greedy choice need not be a *forced* one. Step-wise degeneracy is sufficient for a vanishing gap and not necessary, so a vanishing gap is weaker evidence about a condition than a failed pre-check.

The pre-check of Section 2.6 and the gap are therefore reported as **complementary diagnostics rather than a single test**: the pre-check is cheap, needs no dynamic program, and never rejects a condition that carries headroom, while the gap is complete, certifying exactly when the one-step exact rule cannot be improved on, at the cost of a dynamic program per condition.

> **Corollary 4 (when a learned orderer can matter).** A policy other than the one-step exact rule can improve the objective only if at least one of the following holds: **(a)** the service functional is not exactly evaluable at decision time, for instance because restoration outcomes are stochastic or the network state is only partially observed; **(b)** the action sequence is subject to resource coupling, so that the terminal set is itself a decision and the nested-chain structure of Section 4.2 fails; or **(c)** the objective is determined by the admissibility screen rather than by the ordering, in the sense of Proposition 1.

Corollary 4 is a taxonomy of escape routes from Corollary 2, exhaustive by construction: if the functional is exactly evaluable, the chain is nested, and the screen is not the binding mechanism, then the hypothesis of Corollary 2 applies verbatim. Each of (b) and (c) can therefore be switched on deliberately, and (a) is left open.

> **Remark (relation to constant-factor guarantees).** Adaptive submodularity and the stochastic-depletion literature supply guarantees for greedy and myopic rules on this class of sequential selection problem, typically "at least a constant fraction of the optimum" [31--33]. Those guarantees are *instance-independent*: the same constant holds for every instance, and so cannot distinguish a condition in which ordering has real headroom from one in which it has none. The gap is the complementary object — instance-exact, requiring no policy, sampling or training, at the cost of being available only where the service functional is exactly evaluable on the subset lattice. A study reporting a constant-factor bound alone has not answered whether its benchmark is worth competing on.

### 4.5 The depth profile: value versus accessibility

The gap measures how much is at stake, not whether a controller with bounded foresight can realize it, and the two questions have different answers. Let $\pi_k$ denote a **committed** depth-$k$ plan: at the start of each $k$-step block the controller solves the exact $k$-step problem on the current state, commits to the resulting sequence, executes it, and thereafter reverts to the one-step exact rule. Define the *depth profile*

$$\Gamma_k \;=\; J(\pi_k) - J(\pi^{\mathrm{my}}), \qquad \Gamma_1 = 0, \tag{9}$$

non-decreasing in $k$ and satisfying $\Gamma_m = \Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$. It answers a design question: **how much foresight is needed to capture the value?**

A committed plan is not what a deployed controller does. A **rolling** (receding-horizon) controller replans at every step, executing only the first action of its $k$-step solution; call its realized objective $J^{\mathrm{roll}}_k$. The two controllers coincide only under special conditions, and on the instances studied here they differ sharply:

> **Finding (accessibility of the gap).** On the same instances a committed depth-$k$ plan captures only a minority of $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$ at shallow depths and needs several further steps of foresight to capture all of it, and in one of the two protocols it does not reach the gap at all within the depth horizon tested. A rolling controller captures almost the whole of the same quantity at depth 2 and is exact at depth 3; even at depth 1 it captures more than half.

The *value* of lookahead and the *cost* of collecting it are therefore separate design axes. Section 5.5 measures the profiles for both protocols and for the third test system, and draws the design conclusion.

### 4.6 A cost-aware selection procedure

Combining the two objects with the timing measurements of Section 7 gives a procedure executable before any training.

> **Algorithm 1 (cost-aware selection of a prioritization policy).**
> *Input:* a held-out scenario set; the cost $c_{\mathrm{eval}}$ of one service evaluation; a set $\Pi_h$ of non-learned reference rules and their per-decision costs; candidate policies $\Pi_\ell$.
> 1. Compute $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$ by Proposition 2. If its paired confidence interval over the held-out scenarios contains zero, declare the condition **non-identifiable**, report no ordering comparison, and stop.
> 2. Otherwise compute the depth profile $\Gamma_k$ and the rolling profile to find the smallest depth $k^\star$ whose rolling controller realizes a stated fraction of $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$, and record its cost.
> 3. Compare every candidate on the cost-quality plane against $\pi^{\mathrm{my}}$ and against $\min_{\Pi_h}$ on cost. A candidate $\pi$ is **dominated** if some policy $\pi'$ satisfies $J(\pi') \ge J(\pi)$ and $c(\pi') \le c(\pi)$ with at least one inequality strict.
> 4. Report the bound $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$, the accessibility $k^\star$, and the dominance relation. If every learned candidate is dominated, the correct statement is that learning is unnecessary *for this condition*, and the size of what was forgone is $\Gamma_{\mathrm{LA}}$.

Step 1 is cheap: on the instances used here the dynamic program costs at most a few thousand service evaluations, seconds of wall-clock time, and no simulator rollouts, so the procedure is usable as a benchmark design tool — which is how Section 5.1 first applies it.
